01 / THE PROBLEM

College teaches code, not companies.

Students learn to program, but almost nobody practises running a team. Who do you hire first? What happens when someone takes leave the week before a demo? How do two engineers settle a disagreement, and who decides when to ship?

Business-school simulations are spreadsheets. I wanted a place where you could actually run a small company, with teammates who do real work, make real mistakes and need real decisions from you. Agent Office is that place: an AI startup in a 3D office, with Buddy, my desktop assistant, as its CEO.

02 / WATCH A SPRINT

A desk lamp, from brief to approved spec.

In this clip the founder asks for Lumi, a desk lamp that knows when you’re tired. Buddy walks the brief to the CTO, the team holds a kickoff, and a spec review catches that “tired” meant three different things to three people, before anyone builds.

03 / THE ORG CHART

Roles taken from real startups.

My first version had an “Operations Manager” running everything. That isn’t how a real company works, so I redesigned the org around real roles: the person who plans engineering work and runs quality checks is a CTO, team leads split their team’s work, and operations means budget, parts and schedule.

04 / SPEC BEFORE BUILD

Nobody builds until the founder approves the spec.

My first demo had the CTO handing out tasks straight away, and the team discovering they’d misunderstood each other two days later. That is a lesson worth teaching, but not a process worth copying. The CTO now runs the flow a good startup would:

  1. Kickoff. The goal, the users, the deadline and the budget, agreed with product, both team leads and operations.
  2. Spec. The product manager writes a one-page spec with clear “done when” checks. The leads and operations review it for gaps.
  3. Founder approval. The CTO stops and asks “Approve the spec?”. No building work goes out until the founder says yes.
  4. Leads plan, CTO delegates. Each lead splits their team’s work into tasks with an owner, an output file and a done-when; the CTO sends one brief per task.
  5. Build, review, test. Code goes through review and QA, hardware gets a test plan, and each lead signs off that their team’s pieces fit together.

05 / REAL WORK, NOT ROLE-PLAY

The agents use the tools engineers use.

Each agent is a Claude sub-agent with its own role, instructions and tool permissions. They write real files into the project folder, and they check their work instead of guessing:

  • Firmware is compiled with arduino-cli and PlatformIO before it is reported as done.
  • Circuits are simulated with ngspice; boards are checked and exported with KiCad.
  • Enclosures are modelled in OpenSCAD and sliced with PrusaSlicer to get a real print time.
  • Parts lists come with prices in rupees and Indian suppliers.
  • Your own idea can come in as PDFs, images or notes, dropped onto the office; each project gets its own folder.

06 / THE HUMAN STAYS IN CHARGE

Agents do the work. People approve the risk.

Reading, searching and editing inside the project folder run freely. Anything that could cause harm stops and asks: flashing a board, installing packages, pushing code, deleting files, or spending money. The founder approves from the 3D office, from the terminal, or by voice through Buddy, and every action lands in an execution log.

Why the office is 3D

A log of agent messages is hard to follow. In the office you can see who is working, who is waiting for you, and where work is piling up. Envelopes fly between desks on every hand-off, and stress bars rise in a crunch. The picture explains the process faster than the text.

07 / WHERE IT’S GOING

An open-source startup playground.

The office engine works today. Next, I’m turning it into a playground for students and first-time founders:

  • Pick a project or bring your own. A library of real startup problems, or your own idea and documents.
  • Build your team. Hire roles, let people go, reorganise teams, and live with the consequences.
  • Mix humans and AI. Sit in any seat yourself, with AI teammates who work around you and wait on your output.
  • Real company life. Leave requests, stress, promotion requests and tight deadlines, each one forcing a decision.
  • Find your role. Every decision is logged, so you can look back and see which role really suits you.
  • Low shared cost. The large model does only the real work; cheap rules run the drama, so any student can play.

09 / TAKEAWAYS

Model the process before the agents.

The agents were the easy part. The hard part was the organisation around them: which role owns which decision, when work must stop for a human, and in what order things happen. Getting the org chart and the spec-first flow right made the agents more useful than any prompt change did.
