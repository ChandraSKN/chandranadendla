Unemployment or
Income Stagnation?

Does having a job mean moving ahead?

Five people find work. A headline reports that unemployment has fallen. There is good reason to welcome that news. Those five people now have earnings, and possibly a foothold from which to build a career.

We still know very little about what has changed in their lives. How much does the work pay? How regularly is it available? What does it cost to get to work? Can they meet their commitments and put something aside?

Imagine a labour force of 100 people, with ten unemployed. Five find jobs, reducing unemployment from 10% to 5%. That calculation is identical whether each new job pays ₹12,000 or ₹25,000 a month. For someone with essential expenses of ₹14,000 and no other income, the two jobs produce very different monthly budgets. Both improve earned income from zero. One still leaves a shortfall.

These are fictional amounts, not national averages or estimates of a living wage. Their purpose is to expose a question that a single employment indicator cannot answer: does the work available allow people to build financial security?

Unemployment and income stagnation can exist alongside each other. We need to understand how people enter work, how their earnings develop and how those earnings translate into a life they can afford.

What exactly is the problem?

Unemployment concerns people without work who meet the relevant survey's labour-force criteria. India's Periodic Labour Force Survey, or PLFS, reports the unemployed as a share of the labour force. That denominator matters. The unemployment rate does not describe everyone who lacks a paid job, and it does not measure the adequacy of pay. [1]

Income stagnation describes earnings that make little or no progress over time. Nominal stagnation means the money amount barely changes. Real stagnation means purchasing power barely changes after accounting for prices. A salary can rise in rupees while its purchasing power falls.

Low income is a different issue. Someone's earnings may grow rapidly from a very low starting point and still fail to cover necessities. Another person may have high but stagnant earnings and remain financially comfortable. The starting level and the direction of change both matter.

There is also the problem of insufficient work. A person may want and be available for more paid hours than they can obtain. Skills mismatch is another concern: the available job may make little use of their training. These situations need careful definitions rather than one broad label for every disappointing job.

To see why those distinctions matter, follow three fictional people through a year. Their earnings and expenses are assumptions we can inspect and change. They are not interviews or predictions.

Asha and the cost of waiting

Asha is a graduate looking for her first job. She begins January with ₹72,000 in savings and spends ₹12,000 a month on essentials, including her job search. She has no other income in this example.

During January to March, she keeps applying. Her savings fall to ₹36,000. She is still available for work, but her qualifications cannot pay the next month's expenses by themselves.

April to June consumes the remaining ₹36,000. In July she starts a job with take-home pay of ₹24,000 a month. Her monthly position improves immediately: ₹12,000 can now remain after essentials.

By December, she has rebuilt her savings to ₹72,000. Across the year she earned ₹1,44,000 and spent the same amount. Getting a job was a substantial improvement. It also took the entire second half of the year to restore the money used during her search.

Now replay the year with one change. The same job begins in April. Pay, expenses and starting savings remain unchanged. Three additional months of earnings add ₹72,000, leaving her with ₹1,44,000 at year-end.

The replay does not assume that she suddenly worked harder or found a guaranteed shortcut. It isolates the effect of when an opportunity became available. Her savings made waiting possible. Someone without that buffer would face a different set of choices much earlier.

Ravi and the increment that buys less

Ravi previously took home ₹50,000 a month and spent ₹40,000 on a fixed basket of essentials. His monthly surplus was ₹10,000.

From January, his take-home pay rises by 6% to ₹53,000. Prices for that same basket rise by 8%, taking its cost to ₹43,200. We hold quantities, quality and household responsibilities constant so that the change here comes entirely from prices.

His surplus becomes ₹9,800 a month. Over twelve months, that is ₹1,17,600, compared with ₹1,20,000 under the previous pay and price levels. He earns more rupees and has slightly fewer left over.

The purchasing-power calculation tells a separate part of the story. Dividing the 1.06 salary factor by the 1.08 price factor gives a real pay change of approximately minus 1.85%. That is a comparison against this assumed household basket, not India's official inflation rate.

Replay the year with an 8% pay rise instead. Take-home pay becomes ₹54,000 and the surplus becomes ₹10,800. Both are now 8% above their earlier rupee amounts. Against the same 8% price increase, their purchasing power returns to the earlier level.

This is why the size of an increment needs context. A larger number on a payslip can represent progress, a recovery of lost purchasing power or simply the preservation of an existing standard of living.

Imran and an income he cannot count on

Imran works on contracts at an assumed take-home equivalent of ₹200 per paid hour. He starts with ₹52,000 in savings and has monthly essentials of ₹26,000. Unpaid time generates no income in this model.

His paid hours vary through the year: 160, 140, 120, 160, 100, 140, 180, 120, 100, 160, 140 and 160. He earns ₹3,36,000 across twelve months, averaging ₹28,000 a month. Annual essentials total ₹3,12,000, leaving ₹24,000.

The annual average looks sufficient. Four individual months nevertheless fall short of the essential budget. He uses savings to meet those gaps and finishes with ₹76,000.

Replay exactly the same annual hours and earnings, distributed evenly at 140 paid hours a month. Annual income and year-end savings remain unchanged, but every month's earnings now exceed essentials by ₹2,000.

This change improves predictability without creating additional annual income. It illustrates why income growth and income security deserve separate attention. It does not establish that a client can always smooth work this way or that every worker prefers the same schedule.

The three experiences expose different problems. Asha needs access to work. Ravi needs to understand what his earnings can purchase. Imran needs to manage the timing and reliability of earnings. A useful employment policy, or an employer's response, should identify which problem it is trying to solve.

Why earnings can fail to move ahead

Start with the kinds of work an economy makes available. Training improves what a person can do. Turning that ability into income also requires demand for the work, organisations that can use it productively and opportunities the person can realistically reach. Advising someone to learn another skill leaves the demand side unanswered.

Consider a hypothetical worker qualified for a role in another city. The offer looks attractive, but relocation, rent and a period without income consume the expected gain. The opportunity exists, yet the ability to take it differs across households. Affordable transport, childcare and suitable housing can therefore affect access to better work as well as household expenditure.

Negotiating power also depends on credible alternatives. Someone with several suitable offers has options that a worker with one local employer may lack. Savings, caring responsibilities and the cost of a job search can influence how easily a person can leave. These mechanisms are reasons to investigate a labour market carefully, rather than presume that all workers or employers behave alike.

Productivity is another part of the explanation. Better equipment, organisation and technology can raise output per hour. We should then ask how that additional value reaches workers through compensation, working conditions or future opportunities. An OECD paper published in 2017 documented a gap between productivity and real median compensation growth across many of the OECD countries it studied. Its findings concerned both labour's share of income and the distribution of pay. That historical evidence is a reason to examine how gains are shared, not a direct estimate for India today. [5]

Hours matter too. Monthly pay can rise because someone works longer, even when pay per hour is unchanged. Conversely, higher hourly pay may coexist with flat monthly income if paid hours fall. Looking at only the monthly amount can conceal both changes.

Finally, prices affect what earnings can buy. A lower inflation rate generally means prices are rising more slowly, not that the earlier increases have disappeared. Even when price growth eases, a household may still be adjusting to a higher level of costs.

These mechanisms can interact. A worker with weak alternatives may accept limited pay growth while rising essential costs reduce their ability to save for a future job search. That is a plausible feedback loop to investigate, not a measured explanation for every household.

These averages do not trace the same individuals through time. A change in the mix of workers can move an average even when particular workers receive increases. The figures establish a concern worth examining, while leaving room for substantial differences between people, places and occupations.

We also need care when extending comparisons. MoSPI states that changes to the PLFS design from January 2025 mean annual estimates may not be strictly comparable with earlier reports. A smooth chart assembled across a methodological change can imply continuity that the source itself qualifies. [1]

Why Japan belongs in this discussion

Japan helps us separate measures that are often treated as interchangeable. IMF research published in 2023 found that weak average income growth partly reflected a larger share of part-time workers and declining hours, while real hourly wages had risen across the groups studied. It identified structural issues involving the labour market and incentives affecting second earners. [3]

The IMF's February 2026 mission statement described low unemployment and strong nominal wage growth, alongside pressure on purchasing power as prices outpaced pay. This is a dated assessment, rather than a claim about every subsequent month. [4]

The lesson is a relationship between measures: employment, hourly wages, monthly earnings and purchasing power can move differently. India's institutions, demographics and labour markets require their own explanation. Japan's experience does not establish that India is following the same path.

An argument worth challenging

There are several ways to challenge a claim of stagnation. Are real earnings actually rising for the group and period under discussion? Has the number of paid hours changed? Does compensation include benefits that the cash figure misses? Did an average change because different workers entered the sample?

The wider evidence also includes improvement. The ILO's Global Wage Report 2024–25 materials describe declining global wage inequality in the period examined. A broad statement that nobody benefits from growth would disregard such evidence. [6]

A good article should survive those questions by becoming more precise. It should specify whose earnings, which years and which measure. It should also allow the possibility that the answer is improvement.

What would meaningful progress look like?

My view is that employment should be judged alongside the income and security it provides. A useful picture combines access to work, real earnings, paid hours, predictable payment and protection against disruption. No single score needs to replace those separate measures.

Employers can examine whether higher responsibility and productivity lead to credible progression, whether hours and payments are predictable, and whether benefits reach the people doing the work. Transparent pay criteria make those questions easier to discuss. Financial constraints differ between firms, so a credible response also needs to explain how improvements can be sustained.

For policymakers, the response depends on the problem. More productive jobs and better access to them address opportunity. Effective wage protections and worker representation concern the terms of work. Affordable essential services and social protection can reduce the consequences of a period without earnings. These are proposals to assess against evidence, costs and local conditions, not a claim that one intervention solves every problem.

For an individual, comparing pay, working hours and unavoidable costs can reveal what has actually improved. That is useful for decisions about offers, negotiation or training. It should not turn structural constraints into a judgement about someone's discipline or effort.

Apply the questions to your own position

Compare the same length of time before and after a change. Use take-home income consistently rather than mixing CTC, gross salary and the amount reaching your bank. Treat essential spending, other spending and debt repayments separately where they differ.

A household surplus measures cash flow. It does not by itself measure wealth or purchasing power. An EMI includes principal that may build ownership of an asset. A new dependent changes a household's needs. Moving to a larger home changes what the household consumes. None of those changes should automatically be counted as inflation.

To estimate a price effect, compare the cost of the same basket at two points in time. To understand hours, compare income per paid hour as well as monthly income. The calculator accompanying this article keeps these comparisons separate and makes no assumptions about returns on investments or future salary growth.

How the calculation works

Monthly surplus is take-home income less the two spending inputs. Nominal income growth compares the two income amounts. When a fixed-basket price change is supplied, real income growth is the income ratio divided by the price ratio, minus one. A percentage change from zero income is undefined and appears as “Not comparable”.

Income per paid hour uses the hours you enter. It is not a full measure of compensation or unpaid time. The tool estimates cash flow and an optional price adjustment, not net worth, tax liabilities or financial suitability.

How I used AI for this article

I developed this article with Astra to connect the writing, source review and interactive calculations. The fictional scenarios make the assumptions visible, and the replay controls let readers change a condition and inspect the result. Calculations follow fixed formulas. They do not ask a language model to guess someone's financial future.

The published sources and their limitations sit alongside the argument. The reader's calculator runs in the browser and does not send its entries to an AI service. This is the role I wanted AI to play: help turn a complex question into something people can examine for themselves.

When we celebrate someone finding work, we should also be interested in what happens next. Can they cover essentials reliably? Does greater experience improve their prospects? Can they recover from a setback and make plans with some confidence?

Having a job is an important milestone. The opportunity to build a more secure life is a question we should keep asking after that milestone.
